\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Grading diabetic retinopathy (DR) from colour fundus photographs on the five-level
international scale is difficult for deep networks when the target dataset is small: the cues that separate
adjacent grades are a few small lesions, the grades are ordered, and the classes are imbalanced.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} We present NITSCI, a grading network that couples an EfficientNetV2-S backbone with two
token-level experts: a global expert (a Transformer encoder layer over all spatial tokens) and a lesion expert
(the same tokens re-weighted by a learned, sparsity-regularised lesion-attention map). The experts exchange
information through bidirectional cross-attention and are merged by an image-adaptive gate. A dual head predicts
class probabilities and a continuous ordinal grade, which are combined into one score. The model is pretrained on
APTOS-2019 (\res{naptos} images) and fine-tuned on IDRiD with five-fold cross-validation; decision thresholds are
fitted on out-of-fold predictions only, and the five fold models are ensembled with flip test-time augmentation.
All results are reported on a stratified held-out IDRiD test set (\res{ntest} images) that is never used for
training, model selection or threshold fitting.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} On five-class grading NITSCI reaches an accuracy of \res{acc5}\% (95\% CI
\res{acc5ci}) and a quadratic weighted kappa of \res{qwk5} (95\% CI \res{qwk5ci}); \res{within1}\% of test images are
graded within one level of the reference. For referable DR (grade $\geq 2$) it obtains an AUC of \res{refauc},
sensitivity \res{refsens}\% and specificity \res{refspec}\%. Ablations quantify the contribution of APTOS
pretraining, the lesion expert, cross-attention, the gate, the ordinal head and the inference-time ensemble.
The model has 21.25\,M parameters and needs 23.0\,GFLOPs per $448\times448$ image.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions.} \authnote{One sentence stating the main finding once results are filled, e.g.\ which
components matter most, followed by: ``External validation on independent cohorts is required before any
clinical use.''}
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} diabetic retinopathy; fundus image grading; lesion-aware attention; cross-attention;
mixture of experts; ordinal regression; transfer learning; IDRiD

\paragraph{Highlights}
\begin{itemize}
\item A global expert and a lesion expert exchange information through bidirectional cross-attention.
\item An image-adaptive gate weighs global context against lesion evidence for each image.
\item Classification and ordinal regression heads are fused into one continuous grade score.
\item Thresholds are fitted on out-of-fold data; the test set is never used for any choice.
\item Bootstrap confidence intervals and paired McNemar tests accompany every comparison.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Diabetic retinopathy is a leading cause of preventable vision loss in working-age adults; about one in five
people with diabetes is estimated to have some degree of DR, and the absolute number of affected people is
projected to keep rising through 2045 \citep{teo2021global}. Screening with colour fundus photography is effective,
but grading every image by a trained reader does not scale to the size of the diabetic population.
Deep learning systems have reached specialist-level performance for detecting referable DR when trained on very
large private datasets \citep{gulshan2016development,ting2017development,dai2021deep}, and an autonomous system has
been evaluated in a prospective pivotal trial in primary care \citep{abramoff2018pivotal}.

The situation is different when only a small public dataset is available. The Indian Diabetic Retinopathy Image
Dataset (IDRiD) \citep{porwal2018idrid,porwal2020idrid}, for example, provides a few hundred graded images.
Three properties of the task then become limiting. \emph{First}, adjacent grades on the international scale
\citep{wilkinson2003proposed} are separated by small, sparse findings: a single microaneurysm moves an image from
grade~0 to grade~1, and the count and location of haemorrhages decide between grades~2 and~3. A network that
pools the whole image uniformly can dilute this evidence. \emph{Second}, the labels are ordered, so predicting
grade~4 for a grade-0 image is a much worse error than predicting grade~1, yet standard cross-entropy treats both
equally. \emph{Third}, the grade distribution is imbalanced, and small datasets give unstable estimates of both the
weights and the decision rule.

Prior work addresses these issues separately: lesion-focused models zoom into suspicious regions
\citep{wang2017zoom}, attention modules relate disease-specific features \citep{li2020canet}, ordinal formulations
reduce large errors \citep{niu2016ordinal,cao2020rank}, and pretraining on larger fundus collections or foundation
models improves data efficiency \citep{zhou2023foundation}. In this paper we combine these ideas in one compact
model and, equally importantly, in one evaluation protocol that keeps the test set untouched.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cross-expert lesion-aware architecture.} We propose a head that splits backbone tokens into a global
  expert and a lesion expert, lets each attend to the other, and fuses them with an image-adaptive gate in the
  spirit of mixtures of experts \citep{jacobs1991adaptive,shazeer2017outrageously}. It adds 1.40\,M parameters to
  the backbone.
\item \textbf{Ordinal dual-head decision.} A classification head and a regression head are trained jointly and
  combined into one continuous grade score whose thresholds are fitted on out-of-fold predictions.
\item \textbf{Leakage-free protocol for small datasets.} Cross-dataset pretraining on APTOS-2019 \citep{aptos2019},
  five-fold fine-tuning, a fold-ensemble with test-time augmentation, and a held-out test set that is used once,
  with bootstrap confidence intervals and paired significance tests.
\item \textbf{Component-level evidence.} Architecture, data and inference ablations under identical splits,
  reported for both five-class grading and referable-DR screening.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper reviews related work (Section~\ref{sec:related}), describes the data
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) and the method (Section~\ref{sec:method}), details the experimental setup
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}), reports results (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and discusses findings and limitations
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================
\paragraph{Deep learning for DR grading.}
Early convolutional networks for DR classification \citep{pratt2016convolutional}, and later residual and Inception-style backbones \citep{he2016deep,szegedy2016rethinking}, were followed by large-scale
systems trained on tens of thousands of graded images \citep{gulshan2016development,ting2017development} and by
systems covering the full disease spectrum \citep{dai2021deep}. Public benchmarks include Messidor
\citep{decenciere2014messidor}, DDR \citep{li2019ddr}, APTOS-2019 \citep{aptos2019} and IDRiD
\citep{porwal2018idrid,porwal2020idrid}. More recently, retinal foundation models pretrained with self-supervision
on large unlabelled collections have improved label efficiency \citep{zhou2023foundation}. Our work targets the
complementary setting of a compact supervised model fine-tuned on a small dataset.

\paragraph{Lesion-aware and attention-based grading.}
Zoom-in-Net \citep{wang2017zoom} generates attention maps that localise suspicious regions and re-examines them at
higher resolution, and CANet \citep{li2020canet} uses attention to model relations between DR and diabetic macular
oedema features. Transformers \citep{vaswani2017attention,dosovitskiy2021image,liu2021swin} model long-range
dependencies between image regions. Pixel-level lesion segmentation methods \citep{wu2026helnet} provide explicit
lesion maps but need lesion annotations. NITSCI learns a lesion-attention map \emph{without} lesion masks, using only
grade labels and a sparsity prior, and uses it to define a second token stream rather than only to re-weight a
single pooled feature.

\paragraph{Ordinal formulations.}
Ordinal regression methods exploit label order, e.g.\ by decomposing the problem into binary rank tasks
\citep{niu2016ordinal} with rank-consistent outputs \citep{cao2020rank}. A simpler and widely used alternative in
DR grading is to regress the grade and to optimise the cut-points between grades; quadratic weighted kappa
\citep{cohen1968weighted} is the standard metric that rewards this behaviour. We combine a regression output with
the expected grade of the classification head.

\paragraph{Transfer, ensembling and pre-processing.}
ImageNet pretraining \citep{deng2009imagenet}, including ImageNet-21k \citep{ridnik2021imagenet21k}, is the usual
starting point; an intermediate stage on a larger in-domain dataset narrows the domain gap. Deep ensembles
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple} and weight averaging \citep{polyak1992acceleration} improve accuracy and
calibration. Ben Graham's local-contrast normalisation \citep{graham2015kaggle} and CLAHE
\citep{zuiderveld1994contrast} reduce illumination differences between cameras.

\subsection{Data partitioning}
A stratified 15\% of IDRiD (\res{ntest} images, random seed 42) is set aside as the test set before any training.
It is not used for training, early stopping, model selection, threshold fitting or any hyper-parameter choice.
The remaining \res{ntrainval} images are divided into five stratified folds. For APTOS pretraining a separate
stratified 90/10 split is used for model selection.

\subsection{Pre-processing}
Each photograph is processed identically for both datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:prep}): images whose longer side exceeds
1600 pixels are first down-scaled; the black border is cropped (pixels with grey level $>10$); the image is
zero-padded to a square to preserve the aspect ratio of the fundus disc, and resized to $448\times448$. We then apply
Ben Graham's local-contrast normalisation \citep{graham2015kaggle},
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{ben}} = 4\,I - 4\,(G_{\sigma} * I) + 128,\qquad \sigma = 448/30,
\label{eq:ben}
\end{equation}
where $G_\sigma *$ denotes Gaussian blurring, and set everything outside a centred circle of radius $0.48\times448$
pixels to grey (128). As an alternative we evaluate CLAHE \citep{zuiderveld1994contrast} on the green channel
(clip limit 2, $8\times8$ tiles) followed by bilateral filtering.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview. Given an image $x\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times448\times448}$, a backbone produces
a feature map that is turned into $N$ tokens. Two experts process the tokens, exchange information, and are pooled
and fused by a gate; two heads produce a class distribution and a continuous grade.

\subsection{Backbone and tokenisation}
We use EfficientNetV2-S \citep{tan2021efficientnetv2} pretrained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k
\citep{ridnik2021imagenet21k}, taken from the \texttt{timm} library \citep{wightman2019timm}. Its last stage
(stride 32, 256 channels) gives a $14\times14$ map for a $448\times448$ input. A $1\times1$ convolution with batch
normalisation and GELU projects it to $F\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times h\times w}$ with $D=256$, which is flattened into
$N=hw=196$ tokens $T\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$.

\subsection{Lesion-attention map and lesion expert}
A light head ($3\times3$ convolution to $D/4$ channels, GELU, $1\times1$ convolution to one channel, sigmoid)
predicts a spatial map $m\in[0,1]^{h\times w}$. No lesion annotation is used: $m$ is learned end-to-end from grade
labels and is encouraged to be sparse by an $\ell_1$ penalty (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}). The lesion expert is the
re-weighted token set
\begin{equation}
L = T\odot m, \qquad L_i = m_i\,T_i,\; i=1,\dots,N.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Global expert}
The global expert is one pre-norm Transformer encoder layer \citep{vaswani2017attention} with four heads, a
feed-forward width of $2D$ and dropout 0.1, applied to all tokens: $G = \mathrm{TE}(T)$. It models context across
the whole retina, such as the spatial spread of lesions and their relation to the optic disc and macula.

\subsection{Bidirectional cross-attention}
Each expert queries the other with multi-head attention (four heads, dropout 0.1):
\begin{align}
Z_g &= \mathrm{MHA}(Q{=}G,\;K{=}L,\;V{=}L), & G' &= \mathrm{LN}(G + Z_g),\\
Z_\ell &= \mathrm{MHA}(Q{=}L,\;K{=}G,\;V{=}G), & L' &= \mathrm{LN}(L + Z_\ell).
\end{align}
The global stream thus gathers lesion evidence, and the lesion stream receives the context needed to interpret it.

\subsection{Pooling and adaptive gate}
The global stream is mean-pooled, $v_g = \frac1N\sum_i G'_i$, and the lesion stream is pooled with the lesion
weights, $v_\ell = \sum_i m_i L'_i \big/ (\sum_i m_i + \epsilon)$. A scalar gate decides, per image, how much each
expert contributes:
\begin{equation}
g = \sigma\!\left(w^\top[v_g; v_\ell] + b\right),\qquad
z = \mathrm{GELU}\!\left(g\,W_G v_g + (1-g)\,W_L v_\ell\right).
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
This is a two-expert soft mixture \citep{jacobs1991adaptive}; $g$ is also reported for interpretability.

\subsection{Dual head and grade score}
Two heads share $z$: a classification head (LayerNorm, dropout 0.3, linear) gives logits and probabilities
$p\in\Delta^{4}$, and a regression head of the same form gives $r = f_r(z) + 2$, centred on the middle grade.
Their outputs are combined into a continuous score
\begin{equation}
s = \tfrac12\,\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{4} k\,p_k + \tfrac12\,r .
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
A grade is obtained by comparing $s$ with four ordered thresholds $\theta_1<\dots<\theta_4$:
$\hat y = \#\{j:\ s \geq \theta_j\}$.

\subsection{Training objective}
For a mini-batch the loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE}_{w,\varepsilon}(p, y) + \lambda_r\,\mathrm{SmoothL1}(r, y) + \lambda_m\,\overline{m},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CE}_{w,\varepsilon}$ is cross-entropy with label smoothing $\varepsilon = 0.05$
\citep{szegedy2016rethinking} and class weights $w_c \propto n_c^{-1/2}$ (normalised to mean one, computed on the
training fold), $\overline m$ is the mean of the up-sampled lesion map, $\lambda_r = 0.5$ and $\lambda_m = 0.01$.
The square-root weighting is a compromise between ignoring and fully inverting the imbalance.

\subsection{Two-stage training}
\textbf{Stage 1 (APTOS).} The full model, initialised from ImageNet-21k backbone weights, is trained on APTOS-2019 for
10 epochs (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, EMA decay 0.999). \textbf{Stage 2 (IDRiD).} For each of the five folds, a
model initialised from the stage-1 weights is fine-tuned for at most 25 epochs (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, EMA
decay 0.99, early stopping with patience 8). In both stages we use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with weight
decay $10^{-4}$, a backbone learning rate one third of the head learning rate, a one-cycle cosine schedule with 10\%
warm-up \citep{smith2019super}, mixed precision \citep{micikevicius2018mixed}, gradient clipping at norm 2, and an
exponential moving average (EMA) of weights \citep{polyak1992acceleration} that is used for validation and saved.
The checkpoint with the highest sum of validation accuracy and quadratic weighted kappa (fixed thresholds
$0.5,1.5,2.5,3.5$, no TTA) is kept.

Training augmentation: random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation in $[-180^\circ,180^\circ]$,
translation up to 3\%, scaling in $[0.95,1.10]$, colour jitter (brightness and contrast 0.2, saturation 0.1, hue
0.02), and random erasing \citep{zhong2020random} with probability 0.2.

\subsection{Inference and decision rule}
Each fold model predicts every test image under four views (identity, horizontal flip, vertical flip, both);
probabilities and regression outputs are averaged over views and over the five folds (20 predictions per image).
The decision rule is chosen \emph{on out-of-fold (OOF) predictions only} among three candidates: (i) argmax of $p$;
(ii) rounding $s$ with thresholds $0.5,1.5,2.5,3.5$; (iii) optimised thresholds, found by coordinate search
(four rounds, 80 candidate values per threshold, constrained to remain ordered) maximising OOF accuracy. The chosen
rule was \emph{\res{rule}}, with thresholds (\res{thr}).

\textbf{Referable DR.} For the screening task we use $P(\text{referable}) = p_2 + p_3 + p_4$ and select its
operating threshold on OOF predictions by maximising Youden's $J$ \citep{youden1950index} (selected value
\res{refthr}).

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Implementation}
Experiments were run in PyTorch with \texttt{timm} and scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit} on a single
\res{gpu} GPU, batch size 16. Grad-CAM \citep{selvaraju2017gradcam} visualisations use the
\texttt{pytorch-grad-cam} library \citep{gildenblat2021gradcam}. Random seeds are fixed (42, and $42+k$ for fold
$k$). The number of epochs actually run per fold after early stopping was (\res{epochsrun}).

\subsection{Metrics}
For five-class grading we report accuracy, balanced accuracy, quadratic weighted kappa (QWK)
\citep{cohen1968weighted}, Cohen's kappa, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), macro-averaged precision, recall
and F1, within-one-grade accuracy, macro one-vs-rest ROC AUC and average precision. For referable DR we report
accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value, F1 and ROC AUC.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
95\% confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over test images with 2000 resamples
\citep{efron1993introduction}. Paired comparisons between the full model and each ablation, which share the same
test images, use the two-sided exact McNemar test on per-image correctness \citep{dietterich1998approximate}. With
\res{ntest} test images, differences of a few percentage points are within sampling error; we therefore interpret
point estimates together with their intervals.

\subsection{Compared configurations}
All configurations below use identical splits, seeds, pre-processing (unless varied), augmentation and schedule:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Plain}: EfficientNetV2-S, global average pooling, the same dual head and loss (no experts).
\item \textbf{w/o APTOS}: no stage-1 pretraining (ImageNet-21k initialisation only).
\item \textbf{CLAHE}: CLAHE + bilateral filter instead of Ben Graham normalisation (APTOS stage repeated).
\item \textbf{w/o ordinal head}: $\lambda_r=0$.
\item \textbf{w/o lesion map}: $m\equiv1$, so the lesion expert sees all tokens equally.
\item \textbf{w/o cross-attention}: the experts are not exchanged ($G'=\mathrm{LN}(G)$, $L'=\mathrm{LN}(L)$).
\item \textbf{w/o gate}: fixed $g=0.5$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Main findings}
\authnote{Summarise in three or four sentences, using only claims supported by Tables~\ref{tab:main}--\ref{tab:infer}:
(1) the absolute level of five-class and referable performance with CIs; (2) which ablations had the largest effect;
(3) whether the ordinal head and threshold fitting reduced large errors (within-one-grade accuracy, QWK).}

The protocol choices matter as much as the architecture for a dataset of this size. Fitting thresholds on
out-of-fold predictions rather than on the test set, and selecting the referable-DR operating point the same way,
means that the reported test numbers are not optimistically biased by those choices. The cost is that the OOF
estimates themselves are slightly optimistic, because each fold's checkpoint was selected on the same fold whose
predictions are later used for threshold fitting; the held-out test set is unaffected.

\subsection{Comparison with published results}
Direct numerical comparison with the IDRiD grading challenge \citep{porwal2020idrid} is not possible from this
study, because the challenge uses an official train/test partition whereas we use a stratified random hold-out of the
Kaggle copy. We therefore compare against a plain baseline and ablations trained under identical conditions
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). \authnote{Either (a) re-run the pipeline on the official IDRiD grading partition and add a
table comparing with the challenge results reported in \citet{porwal2020idrid} (copy the numbers from that paper and
cite it), or (b) keep this paragraph as is.}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Small test set.} With \res{ntest} test images, confidence intervals are wide and grade-level metrics for
  rare grades rest on few images.
\item \textbf{Single target dataset.} All results are from IDRiD, which comes from one clinical centre. Performance on
  other populations and cameras (e.g.\ Messidor \citep{decenciere2014messidor} or DDR \citep{li2019ddr}) is untested.
\item \textbf{Non-official split.} The random hold-out prevents direct comparison with the IDRiD challenge.
\item \textbf{Weakly supervised lesion map.} The lesion-attention map is not trained with lesion masks and has not
  been validated against them.
\item \textbf{Single seed.} Variance from training randomness is reflected only through five folds, not through
  repeated runs with different seeds.
\item \textbf{Reference standard.} Grades come from the dataset providers; inter-grader variability is not modelled.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ethical and clinical considerations}
The model is a research prototype and is not a medical device. Both datasets are public and de-identified. Before any
clinical use, the system would need prospective, external validation across populations, cameras and image quality
levels, with subgroup analysis and reporting according to TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024tripod}. In screening, false
negatives delay referral; the operating point should therefore be chosen together with clinicians to reach a target
sensitivity rather than to maximise accuracy.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
We presented NITSCI, a compact DR grading network that combines a global expert and a weakly supervised lesion expert
through bidirectional cross-attention and an adaptive gate, with an ordinal dual head and a two-stage APTOS-to-IDRiD
transfer protocol. Evaluated on a held-out IDRiD test set that was never used for any modelling decision, it reaches
\res{acc5}\% five-class accuracy (QWK \res{qwk5}) and a referable-DR AUC of \res{refauc}. \authnote{One sentence on the
most important ablation finding.} Future work will evaluate the model on the official IDRiD partition and on external
cohorts, validate the lesion-attention maps against pixel-level annotations, and explore retinal foundation-model
backbones \citep{zhou2023foundation}.
